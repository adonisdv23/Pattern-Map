## Final provisional thesis

Some evidence-sensitive AI workflows may benefit from an explicit
responsibility for deciding what context to acquire, identify, preserve,
compare, enrich, admit, withhold, and update before generation. This proposed
responsibility—the discrimination layer—should be inspectable, cost-bounded,
source-aware, correctable, and revisable. Whether making it explicit improves
decisions enough to justify its cost is an empirical question.

Here, `discrimination` means technical differentiation, selection, and
judgment—not social classification or discriminatory treatment. A layer is a
systems responsibility, not necessarily one service, model, prompt, or box.

## Most important claim narrowed or rejected

The component mechanisms are not claimed as new. Retrieval, relevance feedback,
information foraging, provenance, evidence synthesis, source credibility, claim
verification, value of information, RAG, memory, calibration, mixed initiative,
and organizational learning all have established precedents. The plausible
contribution is a boundary-preserving synthesis and evaluation agenda, not
ownership of a new mechanism family.

Related blanket claims were also rejected: `default GPT` is not a stable
baseline; recurrence is not independence; provenance is not correctness;
source authority is not claim support; and neither product case validates the
framework.

## Most important missing evidence

No matched evaluation shows that the full framework improves outcomes enough to
justify its time, token, retrieval, privacy, and review costs. The next serious
evidence would be a preregistered or otherwise pre-specified comparison on a
named task class against strong simpler baselines, with exact units, thresholds,
costs, support errors, missed decisive evidence, harmful inclusion, over-refusal,
human correction, inter-rater reliability, and later decision outcomes.
